Nice work, Kevin — your post is well-structured and I particularly liked how you connected the patient fall scenario to both immediate patient harm and downstream legal liability. That dual framing shows a strong understanding of why accuracy matters in healthcare AI beyond just "getting the right answer."

One hidden risk I noticed in your proposal is the assumption that assigning a document owner and refreshing the RAG index will be sufficient to keep the system current. In practice, there is often a gap between when a policy is approved and when it gets uploaded and re-indexed — this could be days or even weeks depending on workload and IT processes. During that window, the assistant is confidently serving outdated guidance with no indication to the user that a newer version exists. For your patient fall example, imagine the hospital updates its post-fall neuro-check protocol from "every 2 hours" to "every 30 minutes" after a sentinel event, but the RAG index still reflects the old policy. Staff following the assistant's guidance could be unknowingly non-compliant with the new standard of care during that gap period.

One improvement I would suggest is implementing a document expiration flag. Each policy in the corpus could carry a "review by" date, and if that date has passed without a confirmed refresh, the system would automatically append a warning to any answer that cites that document: "Note: This policy was last verified on [date]. Please confirm with your department that it is still current." This simple timestamp check bridges the gap between policy approval and system update without requiring any additional human workflow — it just uses metadata the compliance team already tracks.

Here is my "what if" question for you: What if staff begin relying on the assistant so heavily that they stop reading the actual policy documents themselves? If the system goes offline during a critical incident — say, a network outage during a Code Blue — would staff still know where to locate the physical policy binders or intranet pages and follow the correct procedures without the assistant's help? How would you guard against this kind of institutional knowledge atrophy?
