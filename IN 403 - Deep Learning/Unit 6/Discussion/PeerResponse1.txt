Great post, Victoria — I think the PPE detection system is an excellent choice for this discussion because it highlights how drift can be physical and environmental rather than purely statistical. Your example of new PPE styles or changing lighting conditions is very concrete and easy to visualize, which makes the concept of data drift immediately accessible.

One assumption I noticed in your monitoring plan is that periodic human review will reliably catch the AI's mistakes. In practice, human reviewers are comparing the AI's output against their own judgment — but they can only review what they see. If the camera captures an area where no staff happen to walk during the review window, or if drift only affects edge cases (like a new face shield style that only one department uses), a random sample might miss the problem entirely. There is also a subtler risk: if reviewers are shown the AI's decision first and then asked to confirm or reject it, they may unconsciously anchor to the AI's judgment rather than independently assessing the image — a well-documented phenomenon called automation bias (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). This would make drift harder to catch, not easier, because the human review process itself becomes biased by the system it is supposed to audit.

One improvement I would suggest is supplementing the periodic human review with an automated image distribution check. The hospital could track basic statistical properties of the input images over time — average brightness, color histogram, and resolution — and flag when these properties drift beyond a threshold. This catches environmental changes (lighting, camera repositioning, new uniform colors) automatically and continuously, without waiting for a scheduled human review cycle. It would serve as an early warning that gives the team time to investigate before accuracy actually degrades. Think of it as monitoring the inputs to the model in addition to monitoring the outputs, which creates two layers of protection rather than one.

Here is my "what if" question for you: What if the hospital rotates PPE suppliers every few months based on cost negotiations, meaning the appearance of masks, gowns, and face shields changes frequently and unpredictably? Retraining takes time and resources — would your approach of retraining each time new PPE appears be sustainable, or would you need a fundamentally different strategy (like a model architecture designed to be PPE-style-agnostic)?

References
