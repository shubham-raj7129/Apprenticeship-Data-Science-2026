Great post, Caleb — I appreciate that you connected concept drift specifically to the RAG architecture of the policy assistant. Your insight that the source of drift is likely poor version control or outdated documents in the retrieval corpus is spot-on and shows a strong understanding of how RAG systems differ from traditional models. That distinction matters because fixing drift in a RAG system is fundamentally different from fixing it in a prediction model.

One assumption I noticed in your monitoring plan is that recently changed policies will always be known to the reviewers conducting the test. In practice, policy changes can be subtle or distributed across multiple departments — a compliance officer might know that HIPAA reporting timelines changed, but a floor nurse testing the assistant might not know that the informed consent form was revised last month. If the human reviewers do not have a comprehensive list of every recent change, they will only test what they personally know about, and drift on topics outside their awareness will go undetected. There is also a timing gap risk: between when a policy changes and when someone thinks to test the assistant on that topic, staff could be receiving outdated guidance without anyone realizing it.

One improvement I would suggest is implementing an automated document freshness check rather than relying solely on human testers to discover drift after the fact. Each policy document in the RAG corpus could carry a metadata field for "effective date" and "next review date." The system would automatically flag any document whose review date has passed and either suppress answers that cite stale documents or append a warning: "This answer references a policy last verified on [date] — please confirm it is still current." This shifts the system from reactive (humans discover drift) to proactive (the system alerts when its own knowledge base is potentially outdated), closing the timing gap between policy change and drift detection.

Here is my "what if" question for you: What if the hospital cannot afford to shut down the policy assistant entirely because staff have become dependent on it for daily workflow — dozens of queries per hour during shift changes, for example? A temporary shutdown might mean staff revert to calling supervisors for every policy question, overwhelming the chain of command. Would you consider a middle-ground approach (like switching to "read-only mode" where the assistant only returns direct document excerpts without generating synthesized answers), or do you believe a full shutdown is always the safest option regardless of operational disruption?
