Great post, Miranda — I appreciate how clearly you organized the accountability section with distinct roles for governance, IT, compliance, and policy owners. That separation of concerns is exactly what the NIST AI RMF's GOVERN function calls for, and it avoids the "nobody owns it" problem that plagues many deployed AI systems.

One risk I believe you missed is the silent failure mode unique to RAG systems. Unlike a prediction model that produces a number you can check against ground truth, a RAG assistant can generate a fluent, confident, and completely wrong answer — and there is no automatic error signal. Your monitoring plan catches cases where staff notice and report incorrect answers, but it does not catch cases where staff accept an outdated or incorrect answer because it sounds authoritative. For example, if the informed consent policy was revised last month but the RAG system still retrieves the old version, a staff member might follow the outdated procedure without ever realizing it was wrong. The answer reads perfectly — it just happens to reference a superseded document. This "undetected acceptance" failure is arguably more dangerous than an obvious error because it silently propagates non-compliance without triggering any of your monitoring metrics until an audit or adverse event reveals it.

One additional control I would suggest is implementing an automated document freshness check with version-aware retrieval. Each document in the knowledge base should carry metadata including an effective date, a superseded-by flag, and a next-review-due date. When the system retrieves a chunk from a document whose review date has passed, it should automatically append a warning to the user: "This answer references a policy last verified on [date]. Please confirm it is still current before acting." Additionally, when a policy owner uploads a new version, the system should automatically identify and flag any prior answers (in the audit log) that cited the now-superseded document — enabling the compliance team to proactively review whether any staff acted on outdated guidance during the gap period. This shifts the system from reactive (waiting for someone to notice) to proactive (alerting when its own sources become stale).

Here is my question that could change your decision: What if the retrieval similarity scores are consistently high (meaning the system confidently retrieves documents) but the retrieved documents themselves are outdated — the system does not know a policy was revised because the old version was never removed from the knowledge base? In that scenario, your drift metric (retrieval similarity scores) would show no degradation at all, yet the system would be serving incorrect guidance with high confidence. Would you still recommend "continue with conditions," or does the inability of your primary monitoring metric to detect this specific failure mode warrant a different approach — perhaps mandatory human verification of all answers citing documents older than 90 days?
