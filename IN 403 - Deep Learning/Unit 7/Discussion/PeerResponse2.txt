Good post, Haythem — I think your monitoring approach is practical and well-prioritized. Tracking image brightness and camera angle as drift indicators is a smart choice because those are measurable properties you can automate without needing labeled ground truth. I also appreciate that you explicitly separated "missed violations" (the outcome that matters for safety) from the drift signal (the input-side early warning) — that two-layer approach means you catch problems both before and after they impact performance.

One risk I believe you missed is adversarial adaptation by staff — sometimes called "Goodhart's Law" in monitoring contexts. Once employees know where the cameras are and that an AI is watching, their behavior in monitored zones may no longer represent their actual PPE habits. Staff might put on masks when entering a camera-visible area and remove them in break rooms, supply closets, or patient rooms without cameras. Your system would report near-perfect compliance, leadership would believe the PPE culture is strong, and your "missed violations" metric would stay low — not because violations are not happening, but because they have migrated to locations the model cannot see. The dangerous outcome is a false sense of safety: the hospital thinks PPE adherence is 98% based on AI data, while actual adherence in unmonitored clinical spaces could be significantly lower. This is not a technical model failure — it is a behavioral response to surveillance that no amount of model retraining can fix.

One additional control I would suggest is implementing randomized manual spot audits in unmonitored areas, explicitly compared against the AI's reported compliance rate for the same time period. For example, once a week a trained observer does a 30-minute walkthrough of areas without camera coverage and records PPE compliance. If the manual audit consistently shows 75% compliance while the AI reports 97% in monitored zones, that 22-point gap reveals that the system is measuring compliance theater rather than actual safety behavior. This check is low-cost (one person, 30 minutes, weekly) but provides the one thing the camera system fundamentally cannot: ground truth from locations the model does not observe.

Here is my question that could change your decision: What if you discover that the model's missed violation rate is 4% overall but 12% specifically during night shifts — where lighting is dimmer, staffing is thinner, and the patient population tends to be higher acuity? The aggregate metric looks acceptable, but the subgroup where PPE matters most (high-acuity patients, fewer staff to catch errors) is underserved. Would you still recommend "continue with conditions," or does that subgroup-specific failure warrant pausing the system during night shifts and reverting to manual monitoring for those hours until the model is retrained on low-light images?
