Unit 9 Discussion Post — Regression and Classification

Initial Post

For this week's discussion, I went beyond the course text and used the scikit-learn user guide as my main source, since it is the documentation for the library we use in our Unit 9 code. I also used Breiman's (2001) original random forest paper and three published studies for my real-world examples. Before getting into the models, it helped me to separate the two kinds of problems. Regression predicts a number, like how many uninstalls an app will have tomorrow. Classification predicts a category, like whether a review is good or bad. Four of the five models this week are supervised, meaning they learn from labeled examples. K-means is the exception, which I explain below.

Linear regression is the simplest model of the group. It fits a straight line (or a flat plane when there are several features) by choosing the coefficients that "minimize the residual sum of squares between the observed targets in the dataset, and the targets predicted by the linear approximation" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-d). Its biggest strength is how easy it is to explain. The intercept and coefficient tell a complete story, such as "each extra install adds about this many uninstalls." It is also fast and works well with small datasets. Its main weakness is that it assumes the relationship is a straight line, so it misses curves and thresholds in the data. It can also become unreliable when the features are strongly related to each other. The scikit-learn guide explains that when features are correlated, the estimate "becomes highly sensitive to random errors in the observed target, producing a large variance," which is called multicollinearity (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-d). I would use linear regression for continuous numeric targets where a scatter plot shows a roughly straight trend, and when I need a result that is easy to explain. Our Unit 9 installs-versus-uninstalls data is a good example of this.

A decision tree makes predictions by asking a series of yes or no questions about the features, like a flow chart, until it reaches a leaf that holds the answer. It can be used for both classification and regression. The scikit-learn guide lists several strengths: decision trees are "simple to understand and to interpret," they can be visualized, and they require "little data preparation," since they do not need scaling or normalization the way KNN did in Unit 8 (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-b). They are also a "white box" model, meaning every prediction can be explained by the rules that led to it. The weaknesses are just as clear. A tree can grow "over-complex trees that do not generalize the data well," which is overfitting, and it can be "unstable because small variations in the data might result in a completely different tree being generated" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-b). The guide also notes that tree predictions are "piecewise constant," so trees "are not good at extrapolation" outside the range of the training data. I would use a decision tree for tabular data with a mix of feature types, especially when people need to read and trust the rules, such as in medical or business decision making.

A random forest fixes many of the decision tree's weaknesses by building many trees and combining them. Breiman (2001) introduced the method, where each tree is trained on a random sample of the data and considers a random subset of features when splitting. The scikit-learn guide explains that each tree is built from "a bootstrap sample" drawn with replacement, and that this randomness is meant "to decrease the variance of the forest estimator" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-c). Because each tree makes different mistakes, "by taking an average of those predictions, some errors can cancel out" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-c). One detail I found interesting is that while the original method lets each tree vote, scikit-learn actually "combines classifiers by averaging their probabilistic prediction, instead of letting each classifier vote for a single class" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-c). The strengths of a random forest are high accuracy, resistance to overfitting, and a built-in measure of which features matter most. The weaknesses are that it is slower to train and predict than a single tree, takes more memory, and gives up the simple readable rules that made one tree so easy to explain. I would use a random forest on medium to large tabular datasets, for either classification or regression, when accuracy matters more than explainability.

K-means is different from the other four because it is unsupervised. It does not need labels. Instead, it groups data into K clusters by assigning each point to the nearest cluster center and moving each center to the average of its points until the centers settle. The scikit-learn guide notes that it "scales well to large numbers of samples," which is a major strength (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-a). Its weaknesses are that it "requires the number of clusters to be specified" ahead of time, and that it assumes clusters are "convex and isotropic," meaning roughly round and similar in size, so it "responds poorly to elongated clusters, or manifolds with irregular shapes" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-a). It can also get stuck in a poor solution depending on where the centers start, which is why scikit-learn uses the k-means++ starting method and runs the algorithm several times. In high-dimensional data, distances become less meaningful, and the guide suggests running PCA first, which connects to this week's reading on principal component analysis (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-a). Since K-means relies on distance, the features should also be scaled first. I would use K-means on numeric, unlabeled data when the goal is to discover groups, such as customer segments.

K-nearest neighbors (KNN), which we used in Unit 8, classifies a new point by looking at the K closest training points and taking a majority vote. It can also be used for regression by averaging the neighbors' values. The scikit-learn guide describes it as "instance-based learning," because it "does not attempt to construct a general internal model, but simply stores instances of the training data" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-e). This is a strength because training is almost instant and the model can fit very irregular boundaries. However, it is also a weakness because all the work happens at prediction time, and a brute-force search "quickly becomes infeasible" as the number of samples grows (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-e). The choice of K is also important. The guide explains that "a larger k suppresses the effects of noise, but makes the classification boundaries less distinct" (scikit-learn developers, n.d.-e). Like K-means, KNN depends on distance, so it needs scaled features and becomes less effective in high-dimensional data. I would use KNN on small to medium datasets with a few well-scaled numeric features, such as simple recommendation systems or pattern matching.

To conclude, I looked at three real-world cases where these models have been applied successfully. The first is a random forest in the Microsoft Kinect. Shotton et al. (2011) trained "a deep randomized decision forest classifier" on hundreds of thousands of synthetic depth images to label each pixel of a person's body by body part, so the system could track a player's joints. Their implementation ran "under 5ms per frame (200 frames per second) on the Xbox 360 GPU," and the authors state that the algorithm "forms a core component of the Kinect gaming platform" (Shotton et al., 2011). This shows how a random forest can be both accurate and fast enough for millions of consumers. The second is K-means in medicine. Ahlqvist et al. (2018) used k-means and hierarchical clustering on six clinical variables from 8,980 newly diagnosed patients in Sweden and found five replicable subgroups of adult-onset diabetes. The groups had very different risks. For example, the most insulin-resistant cluster had a significantly higher risk of diabetic kidney disease than two of the other clusters, even though those patients had been prescribed similar diabetes treatment (Ahlqvist et al., 2018). Since K-means needs no labels, it found groups that the traditional type 1 and type 2 labels had missed. The third is a decision tree in emergency medicine. Goldman et al. (1982) used recursive partitioning, a decision tree method, to build "a decision protocol in the format of a simple flow chart" that identifies heart attacks in patients with chest pain using nine clinical factors. When tested on 468 patients at a second hospital, the protocol performed as well as the physicians, and combining it with physician judgment raised specificity from 67% to 77% (Goldman et al., 1982). This example shows the value of a decision tree's readability, since doctors can follow a simple flow chart quickly in an emergency room. Together, these cases show me that the "best" model depends on the goal: speed and accuracy for the Kinect, discovering hidden groups for diabetes, and clear explainable rules for emergency care.
