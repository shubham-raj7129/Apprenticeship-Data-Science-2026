# Unit 1 Discussion: Botnets

## Initial Post

A botnet is a network of compromised computer devices, often called "zombie computers," that have been infected with malware and are remotely controlled by a malicious actor known as a "bot herder" (Kaspersky, 2024). The term itself is a combination of "robot" and "network," reflecting how these hijacked machines operate as automated agents carrying out tasks—such as distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks, spam campaigns, data theft, and cryptocurrency mining—without their owners' knowledge. What makes botnets particularly dangerous is their scale: a single bot herder can control thousands or even millions of devices simultaneously, amplifying the impact of any attack far beyond what a single machine could achieve.

Botnets are built upon two primary architectural models. The first is the **client-server (centralized) model**, in which all zombie computers connect to a single command-and-control (C2) server operated by the bot herder. The bot herder issues instructions through this central server, which relays commands to every infected device. While this model is straightforward to manage, it has a critical weakness: the central server represents a single point of failure, meaning that if law enforcement or security researchers identify and seize the server, the entire botnet can be disrupted. The second model is the **peer-to-peer (P2P, decentralized) model**, in which there is no central server at all. Instead, each zombie computer functions as both a client and a server, propagating commands laterally across the network (Kaspersky, 2024). This architecture is far more resilient because there is no single node to take down, and it makes it significantly harder to trace the identity of the bot herder. P2P botnets have become increasingly common in recent years due to these advantages.

At the core of every botnet is its **command-and-control (C2) protocol**, the communication channel through which the bot herder sends instructions to the zombie computers. Historically, many botnets relied on **IRC (Internet Relay Chat)** protocols, where infected machines would join a specific IRC channel and listen for commands sent by the bot herder. However, because IRC traffic is relatively easy to detect and block, modern botnets have shifted toward **HTTP/HTTPS-based** C2 channels, which blend in with normal web traffic, making detection considerably harder. Some botnets use **DNS-based** communication or even leverage legitimate platforms like social media and cloud services to relay commands, further obscuring their activity from network security tools. The zombie computers themselves are any internet-connected device—desktops, laptops, smartphones, IoT devices like smart cameras and routers—that has been infected, typically through phishing emails, drive-by downloads, or exploitation of software vulnerabilities. Once compromised, the malware grants the bot herder administrative-level control over the device, allowing them to steal data, install additional malware, monitor activity, or mobilize the device for coordinated attacks.

Two widely known tools associated with botnets are **Mirai** and **Zeus**. Mirai is an open-source malware that specifically targets IoT devices running Linux by scanning for devices that still use factory-default usernames and passwords, then infecting them and recruiting them into a botnet used primarily for massive DDoS attacks. Mirai gained notoriety in 2016 when it was used to launch one of the largest DDoS attacks in history against the DNS provider Dyn, disrupting access to major websites. Zeus (also known as Zbot), on the other hand, is a Trojan horse that primarily targets Windows machines and is designed for financial theft. It uses keystroke logging and form-grabbing techniques to steal banking credentials, and its infected machines form a botnet that the operator can control for data exfiltration and further malware distribution.

In conclusion, despite their differences in architecture and purpose, most botnets share several common features. They all rely on some form of C2 infrastructure to coordinate the actions of zombie computers. They are built through covert infection of devices, often exploiting vulnerabilities or user negligence. They are designed for scalability, allowing the bot herder to grow the network continuously. And they are inherently stealthy, using techniques like encryption, traffic obfuscation, and domain generation algorithms to avoid detection by security tools (Kaspersky, 2024). Understanding these characteristics is essential for developing effective defenses against what remains one of the most persistent threats in cybersecurity.
